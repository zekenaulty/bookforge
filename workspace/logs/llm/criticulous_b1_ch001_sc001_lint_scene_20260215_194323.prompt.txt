Artie’s consciousness slips away as the hospital room monitor emits a long, flat tone. The antiseptic scent, once a sharp reminder of his failing organs, begins to thin, replaced by a strange, ozone-heavy void. The dimming hospital lights flicker one last time, casting long, tired shadows across the linoleum before the world simply stops trying to exist.

He was dying. He knew he was dying because the last thing he felt was the frantic, scratchy texture of a Crinkled Dollar Bill clutched in his right hand. It was his emergency vending machine money—the last remnant of a life spent calculating the cost of a mid-afternoon granola bar. He’d spent thirty years as a cog in a machine that didn't even bother to oil itself, and now, the machine was finally shutting down.

The transition wasn't a tunnel of light or a choir of angels. It was a file transfer.

Artie’s eyes snap open to find himself standing in a featureless white expanse. There was no ceiling, no walls, just a blinding white floor that stretched toward an infinite horizon. The silence of the hospital was gone, replaced by a low-frequency digital hum that vibrated in the marrow of his bones.

"Great," Artie muttered, his voice sounding oddly crisp in the void. "The afterlife is a lightbox. I’m dead and I’ve been sent to the one place with worse lighting than the accounting department."

He looked down at himself. He was still wearing the thin, indignity-inducing hospital gown, but the crushing weight in his chest—the pneumonia, the heart failure, the general exhaustion of existing—had vanished. He felt light. Too light. He checked his right hand. The Crinkled Dollar Bill was still there, as stubborn as his own cynicism. He reached into the pockets of the gown—which shouldn't have had pockets, but apparently the afterlife had better tailoring—and found his Leather Wallet and his Employee ID Badge.

"Candidate four million?" Artie sighed, rubbing his hazel eyes. "I couldn't even be in the top thousand? Typical. I bet the guys in the first million got a better starting zone. Probably something with a beach and a bar."

"Ten health," Artie noted, his eyes narrowing as he read the floating stats. "That seems low. Is that low? I feel like that’s the kind of number that gets you killed by a stiff breeze or a particularly aggressive squirrel."

The void around him began to pulse. The infinite horizon shimmered, the white light fragmenting into millions of tiny, floating pixels that swirled like a digital snowstorm.

[Tutorial Initialization Commencing.]

"Survive," Artie repeated, a smirk playing on his lips despite the absurdity of it all. "Well, I’ve been doing that for twenty-eight years on a salary that wouldn't buy a decent steak. How much harder can the System be?"

The blue window expanded, filling his vision with a blinding flash of data.
