Artie’s stomach growled, a hollow, echoing rumble that cut through the sterile quiet of the office breakroom. He was aggressively hungry, the kind of mid-afternoon starving that made the humming fluorescent lights overhead seem to pulse in time with his mounting headache. It was exactly three-forty-two in the afternoon on a Tuesday, the absolute nadir of the corporate workweek. The spreadsheets he had been staring at for the last six hours had blurred into a meaningless matrix of black numbers on a white background, a digital purgatory from which there was no escape. Dragging his feet across the scuffed linoleum, his muddy brown eyes locked onto the glowing, humming monolith in the corner: the vending machine. It was a beacon of high-sodium salvation.

The breakroom of Miller, Stanton, and Associates was a depressing testament to mid-sized accounting firm culture. It smelled perpetually of burnt coffee and stale microwave popcorn. The walls were painted a color that could only be described as "despair beige," and the single window looked out onto a brick wall of the adjacent building. But Artie didn't care about the aesthetics. He cared about the calories. He needed fuel. His skinny-fat physique required sustenance to maintain its delicate balance of lethargy and anxiety. 

He ran a hand through his messy brown hair, adjusting the collar of his cheap, ill-fitting suit. The polyester blend was itchy against his pale skin, a constant reminder of his meager salary. Around his neck, his Employee ID Badge swung on its lanyard, a plastic pendulum marking the slow death of his late twenties. He reached into his right pocket and pulled out his Leather Wallet, his thumb flicking it open to extract a slightly crumpled dollar bill. It was his last physical dollar, a relic in an increasingly cashless society, saved specifically for this moment. He shoved the Leather Wallet back into his pocket, keeping his right hand free to smooth out the currency.

"Come on," Artie muttered, feeding the dollar into the machine's blinking slot. The bill validator whirred, a mechanical grinding sound that sounded like a robot clearing its throat. It spat the bill out with a dismissive buzz, demanding a flatter sacrifice. 

Artie sighed. He rubbed the bill against the sharp edge of the machine, trying to iron out the wrinkles with friction. He tried again. The machine swallowed it, thought about it for three agonizing seconds, and spat it out again. 

"Don't do this to me," Artie whispered, his voice cracking. He flipped the bill over, smoothing it against his thigh. He fed it in upside down. The machine whirred. It clicked. It swallowed the cash with a satisfying mechanical clunk. The digital display lit up with a glorious $1.00.

He punched in the coordinates for the barbecue potato chips. E-4. The alpha-numeric code of champions. It was the only acceptable snack in the entire machine. The pretzels were too dry, the chocolate was always slightly chalky, and the generic cheese crackers tasted like salted cardboard. But the barbecue chips? They were a symphony of artificial flavoring and grease. 

Inside the glass case, the metal coil began its slow, agonizing rotation. The silver packaging of the chips shimmied forward, reaching the precipice of the shelf. Artie leaned in, his pale face reflecting in the glass, anticipating the dull thud of delivered processed carbs. He could almost taste the smoky, salty dust on his fingers. 

The bag tipped. It slid. And then, defying all laws of physics and basic human decency, it caught on the lip of the shelf below. The coil stopped turning. The bag of chips hung suspended at a forty-five-degree angle, mocking him. 

Artie blinked. He tapped the glass with his knuckles. The stuck snack did not budge. He pressed his forehead against the cool pane, staring at the foil bag trapped just inches from freedom. His ten out of ten maximum stamina felt entirely drained by the sheer injustice of it all. He wasn't about to walk away and let the next guy get a two-for-one drop. 

He stepped back, rolling his shoulders. He glared at the stuck snack, weighing his options. Banging on the glass hadn't worked. Shaking the machine was risky; there were warning labels about tipping hazards, and he didn't want to be the guy who died for a bag of chips. But leaving was out of the question. He had invested his last dollar. He had invested his emotional well-being. 

He made his decision. If the machine wouldn't yield its treasure willingly, it was time for manual retrieval. He rolled up the sleeves of his cheap suit jacket, his eyes narrowed with determination. The dispenser flap at the bottom looked wide enough to accommodate his arm. It was a risk, sure. But it was a calculated risk. And Artie was, after all, an accountant.
