It was his emergency vending machine money—the last remnant of a life spent calculating the cost of a mid-afternoon granola bar. He’d spent thirty years as a cog in a machine that didn't even bother to oil itself, and now, the machine was finally shutting down.

The transition wasn't a tunnel of light or a choir of angels. It was a file transfer.

Artie’s eyes snap open to find himself standing in a featureless white expanse. There was no ceiling, no walls, just a blinding white floor that stretched toward an infinite horizon. The silence of the hospital was gone, replaced by a low-frequency digital hum that vibrated in the marrow of his bones.

"Great," Artie muttered, his voice sounding oddly crisp in the void. "The afterlife is a lightbox. I’m dead and I’ve been sent to the one place with worse lighting than the accounting department."

He was still wearing the thin, indignity-inducing hospital gown, but the crushing weight in his chest—the pneumonia, the heart failure, the general exhaustion of existing—had vanished. He felt light. Too light. He checked his right hand. The Crinkled Dollar Bill was still there, as stubborn as his own cynicism.
