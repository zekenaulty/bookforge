Artie stands before the breakroom vending machine, his last five dollars and his sanity on the line. The fluorescent lights overhead hummed with a headache-inducing frequency, casting a sickly yellow glow over the cracked linoleum floor. He smoothed the crinkled edges of the five-dollar bill against his thigh, his hazel eyes narrowed in a mix of desperation and pure, unadulterated spite. 

"Just give me the bag of salt-and-vinegar chips and nobody gets hurt," Arthur 'Artie' Penhaligon muttered, his voice raspy from a day spent explaining to middle-management why the printer couldn't 'smell' the lack of toner.

He fed the bill into the slot. The machine made a sound like a blender full of gravel, shuddered, and spat the money back out. Artie caught it before it hit the floor, his slender frame tensing. He wasn't a man built for physical confrontation—his mousy brown hair was unruly and uncombed, his pale skin spoke of a life lived entirely under the glow of a monitor, and his most prominent physical feature was a faint keyboard callus on his right palm. But today, the vending machine was the final boss of a very long, very terrible Tuesday.

He tried again. This time, the machine swallowed the bill with a reluctant groan. Artie didn't hold his breath; he knew better. He watched the digital display flicker through its options before settling on a blinking '0.00'. 

"No," Artie whispered. "No, you did not just eat my last five dollars."

He punched the buttons for C-4. To his surprise, the metal coil began to turn. It rotated slowly, agonizingly, pushing the blue-and-silver bag toward the edge of the glass. Then, it stopped. The snack hung by a single, plastic corner, dangling over the abyss like a climber who had lost their grip. It just swayed, mocking him.

"You've got to be kidding me." Artie leaned his forehead against the cool glass. 

But Artie wasn't ready to give up. He reached down into the retrieval flap, his hand pushing past the heavy plastic guard. He could see the bag. If he could just reach high enough, maybe he could give the coil the nudge it needed. As he reached, he noticed a small puddle of spilled diet soda pooling near the base of the machine. He also didn't notice the frayed copper wire peeking out from the back of the unit, vibrating with the same rhythmic intensity as the buzzing lights.

"Just... one... inch..." 

His fingers brushed the crinkly plastic. He hooked his index finger around the corner and gave a sharp tug. The bag of salt-and-vinegar chips gave way, falling into his right hand with a satisfying thud. 

"Ha! Victory is mine, you piece of—"

The hum of the machine changed. The low-frequency buzz spiked into a high-pitched whine that vibrated in Artie's teeth. A blue spark jumped from the frayed wire to the soda puddle, and then, with the speed of a system crash, it found Artie.

The world dissolved into static and high-voltage blue light as the surge hit.

The pain wasn't even pain; it was just noise. It was the sound of a thousand dial-up modems screaming at once inside his skull. Artie felt his muscles lock, his vision exploding into a kaleidoscope of error messages and digital artifacts. He couldn't even scream. His grip tightened on the bag of salt-and-vinegar chips, the plastic crinkling one last time before his nervous system simply gave up the ghost.

The breakroom, the smell of burnt coffee, and the soul-crushing weight of his ninety-to-five existence all vanished. The floor beneath his feet didn't just disappear; it ceased to be a concept. 

Artie drifted. He felt weightless, a single line of corrupted code floating in a sea of unrendered data. The blue light faded, replaced by an endless, blinding expanse of white. There were no walls, no ceiling, just a horizon that stretched on forever, dotted with floating geometric shapes that looked like the building blocks of a world that hadn't finished loading.

He looked down at his hand. He was still holding the bag of salt-and-vinegar chips, though it looked strangely pixelated around the edges now.

"Well," Artie said, his voice echoing in the vast silence of the void. "At least I got the chips."
